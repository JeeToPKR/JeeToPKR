If you like Notion but have started feeling that it is too cloud-dependent, too busy, or simply not as private as you would prefer, you are not alone. The appeal of a local-first productivity app is pretty simple: your notes, projects, documents, and knowledge base should not have to live entirely on somebody else’s servers. That is where open-source and privacy-focused Notion alternatives become interesting.

Instead of putting everything behind one SaaS platform, tools such as AppFlowy, AFFiNE, Anytype, Logseq, Focalboard, SiYuan, and Trilium Notes give you different levels of local storage, offline access, self-hosting, encryption, customization, and data ownership.

I looked at these tools from the perspective of someone actually trying to replace Notion—not just checking feature lists. The big question is not “Which app has the most features?” It is “Which one fits the way you actually work?”

Quick answer: For a Notion-like experience with open-source roots, AppFlowy and AFFiNE are the closest starting points. Anytype is particularly interesting if privacy and local-first architecture matter most, while Logseq is better suited to people who think in outlines, backlinks, and knowledge graphs.

The Top 7 Open-Source Notion Alternatives

1. AppFlowy — The Closest Open-Source Notion Alternative

AppFlowy is probably where I would start if your primary complaint is: “I like Notion, but I want more control over my data.” It is designed around the familiar workspace model—pages, databases, projects, wikis, and collaboration—while also providing local installation and self-hosting options. AppFlowy's documentation specifically positions it as an open-source Notion alternative with local installation, offline support, customization, and data control.

The current AppFlowy ecosystem also supports cloud and self-hosted deployments, with the company highlighting on-premises, local AI, air-gapped environments, and data sovereignty for organizations.

Why AppFlowy Stands Out: The biggest advantage is familiarity. If you've spent years building Notion databases, pages, project trackers, and documentation, switching to something based around completely different concepts can be frustrating. AppFlowy tries to preserve that general productivity-workspace feeling.

It also supports:

  • Pages and blocks
  • Databases
  • Project management
  • Team workspaces
  • AI features
  • Offline use
  • Desktop and mobile apps
  • Self-hosting
  • Customization

AppFlowy's current cloud offering has a free tier, while its self-hosted offering provides deployment options for users who want control over their infrastructure.

My Hands-on Verdict: AppFlowy makes the most sense for a Notion user who doesn't want to relearn everything. Here is the catch: self-hosting is not the same as installing a desktop app. Once you start running the collaborative server infrastructure yourself, you inherit responsibilities such as updates, backups, authentication, and server maintenance. For developers and technically comfortable teams, that's a reasonable trade. For someone who simply wants a notes app, the local desktop application is the easier route.

  • Pros: Very Notion-like workflow, Open-source components, Offline support, Self-hosting options, Databases and project management, Cross-platform support, Suitable for teams, Strong data-control story.
  • Cons: Some advanced functionality depends on the broader AppFlowy ecosystem, Self-hosting requires technical maintenance, Cloud and self-hosted offerings have different considerations, It can feel more complex than a simple markdown notebook.

Best for: People who want “Notion, but with more control.”

2. AFFiNE — Best Notion + Miro Combination

If AppFlowy feels like a Notion replacement, AFFiNE feels like someone combined Notion and Miro and then added a privacy-first architecture. AFFiNE combines documents, databases, planning, and a visual whiteboard inside one workspace. Its desktop application is explicitly local-first and can work fully offline without requiring an account.

AFFiNE also provides official self-hosting documentation and describes itself as an open-source, self-hosted knowledge base deployable through Docker.

What Makes AFFiNE Different? The Edgeless/whiteboard experience is the thing to pay attention to. Instead of forcing everything into a conventional document structure, AFFiNE lets you mix written content and visual thinking. That can be useful for:

  • Product planning
  • Mind maps
  • Research
  • Brainstorming
  • Project planning
  • Visual documentation
  • Knowledge management

The desktop app stores documents and whiteboards locally, while cloud synchronization is optional.

My Hands-on Verdict: AFFiNE is one of the more interesting choices if you frequently jump between “I need to write this” and “I need to draw this out.” The trade-off is that a feature-rich visual workspace can feel heavier than a straightforward markdown application. If your workflow consists mostly of daily notes and text files, some of AFFiNE's flexibility may be unnecessary.

  • Pros: Open source, Local-first, Strong offline support, Documents + whiteboards, Self-hosting available, Modern interface, Markdown support, Good fit for visual thinkers.
  • Cons: More complex than a basic notes application, Some cloud features are separate from the local experience, Collaboration workflows can be more involved than Notion's mature SaaS model.

Best for: People who want documents, databases, and whiteboards in one privacy-conscious workspace.

3. Anytype — Best for Privacy and Local-First Work

If privacy is your number-one requirement, Anytype deserves serious attention. Anytype describes itself as a local-first, peer-to-peer, end-to-end encrypted knowledge OS. Its architecture is designed around keeping information on the user's devices rather than making a centralized cloud service the default. This is a fundamentally different approach from the typical SaaS productivity application.

How Anytype Works: Instead of thinking primarily in terms of pages inside a cloud workspace, Anytype gives you objects, types, relations, databases, and spaces. You can build:

  • Notes
  • Tasks
  • Journals
  • Wikis
  • Databases
  • Kanban boards
  • Calendars
  • Custom information systems

Its documentation says data lives on the device first, can work offline, and can synchronize between devices using its encrypted architecture.

My Hands-on Verdict: Anytype is the tool I would investigate if your reaction to Notion is: “Why does my private knowledge base need to depend on a company's cloud?” The learning curve is the price you pay. Anytype's object-based model is powerful, but initially it can feel less intuitive than simply creating a page and dragging blocks around. Once the underlying concepts click, however, it becomes much easier to build a personal information system rather than just a collection of documents.

  • Pros: Local-first, Offline-first, End-to-end encryption, Peer-to-peer synchronization, Databases and relations, Highly customizable, Cross-platform, Strong privacy architecture.
  • Cons: Different mental model from Notion, More to learn initially, Some users may find object-based organization unfamiliar, Collaboration isn't the primary reason to choose it over mature SaaS products.

Best for: Privacy enthusiasts, researchers, developers, and people building a personal knowledge operating system.

4. Logseq — Best for Outliners, Backlinks & Knowledge Graphs

Logseq is not really trying to become another Notion clone. That's exactly why I like it. Logseq is a local-first, non-linear outliner notebook designed around journals, outlines, linked references, and personal knowledge management. Its project describes itself as open source and free for personal use. Instead of starting with a giant blank page, Logseq encourages you to work with bullets and linked blocks.

Why Developers Like Logseq: Imagine you are researching a technical topic. You write:

- Kubernetes
  - Pods
  - Services
  - Networking

Then you link concepts across different notes. Over time, those links create a knowledge graph. That workflow can be fantastic for developers, researchers, students, writers, technical documentation, daily journaling, and second-brain systems.

My Hands-on Verdict: Logseq is one of those tools where the first hour can feel slightly weird and the next six months can feel completely natural. If you naturally think in bullet points, you'll probably understand it quickly. If you want polished databases, drag-and-drop dashboards, and a conventional project-management interface, however, Logseq may feel too opinionated.

There is also an important caveat around synchronization. Logseq's Sync has historically been developed separately from its core local-first experience, and official documentation notes that Sync can be self-hosted; its earlier beta documentation warned that Sync was not yet suitable for simultaneous collaboration and recommended backups.

  • Pros: Local-first, Open source, Excellent offline workflow, Powerful backlinks, Graph-based knowledge management, Markdown/Org-mode friendly workflows, Excellent for journaling, Free for personal use.
  • Cons: Not a direct Notion replacement, Less database-centric, Collaboration is not its strongest point, Steeper learning curve for conventional productivity users.

Best for: People who want a personal knowledge graph rather than another cloud workspace.

5. Focalboard — Best for Simple Kanban Project Management

Focalboard is worth mentioning because it takes a different route. Rather than attempting to replicate every aspect of Notion, Focalboard focuses heavily on boards, cards, projects, and task management. It has existed as an open-source, self-hosted alternative to tools such as Trello, Notion, and Asana, with desktop and server editions.

Here's the Catch: There is a major 2026 caveat. The standalone Focalboard repository currently states that it is not maintained, and Mattermost announced that development of the Focalboard plugin was moving away from active feature development. So while Focalboard can still be interesting for existing installations, I would not treat it like a fast-moving modern productivity platform.

My Hands-on Verdict: If you already have Focalboard running and it does everything you need, there may be little reason to abandon it. But if you're starting from zero in 2026, its maintenance status should be part of your decision.

  • Pros: Open source, Self-hostable, Kanban boards, Simple project management, Desktop versions, Familiar card-based workflow.
  • Cons: Standalone repository is currently not maintained, Development has slowed significantly, Smaller ecosystem, Not a full Notion replacement.

Best for: Existing Focalboard users and people who specifically want a simple self-hosted board.

6. SiYuan — A Serious Block-Based Knowledge Base

SiYuan takes the block-based approach much further than a basic note-taking app. It supports block references, backlinks, databases, flashcards, Markdown, templates, plugins, and privacy-focused local storage. Its official site describes it as a privacy-first personal knowledge management system. One thing I particularly like about SiYuan's architecture is that your workspace is stored locally. The documentation says document data is stored in .sy files using JSON within the workspace structure.

Why SiYuan Is Interesting: SiYuan is particularly appealing if you want block-level references, bidirectional links, rich documents, flashcards, databases, local storage, mobile access, Docker deployment, and end-to-end encrypted synchronization. It also offers Windows, macOS, Linux, Android, iOS/iPadOS and server deployment options.

My Hands-on Verdict: SiYuan feels more like a serious personal knowledge database than a casual notes app. The downside is exactly the same thing that makes it powerful: there are a lot of concepts to understand. If you want something where you can open the app and immediately start writing a simple note, SiYuan can feel more technical. Its basic local storage is free, while certain synchronization and cloud-related capabilities are paid.

  • Pros: Fully open source, Excellent local storage, Block references, Bidirectional links, Databases, Flashcards, Offline operation, Self-hosting, E2EE synchronization, Mobile support.
  • Cons: Learning curve, Interface can feel technical, Some cloud features are paid, More focused on personal knowledge management than team collaboration.

Best for: Power users who want block-level control over a private knowledge base.

7. Trilium Notes — For Huge Personal Knowledge Bases

If your notes have become a gigantic tree of projects, research, code snippets, documents, and reference material, Trilium Notes is worth investigating. Trilium is a free and open-source hierarchical note-taking application designed specifically around large personal knowledge bases. Its current TriliumNext project supports desktop and server deployments and provides features such as note versioning, full-text search, scripting, relation maps, encryption, and self-hosted synchronization.

What Makes Trilium Different? Its hierarchical structure is the star. You can create deeply nested note structures, clone notes into multiple locations, add attributes, link information, and build a large personal knowledge system. It also includes rich WYSIWYG editing, tables, images, math, code syntax highlighting, full-text search, versioning, relation maps, note attributes, scripting, REST API, web clipping, Markdown import/export, and self-hosted synchronization.

My Hands-on Verdict: Trilium makes sense when organization itself is part of your workflow. For example, imagine a developer maintaining separate trees for clients, programming languages, infrastructure, research, personal projects, and documentation. Trilium's hierarchy can handle that style extremely well. The trade-off is that it doesn't feel as immediately modern or collaboration-oriented as Notion.

  • Pros: Free and open source, Excellent hierarchical organization, Local desktop application, Self-hosting, Large knowledge-base support, Strong search, Version history, Encryption, Scripting/API support.
  • Cons: More traditional interface, Less collaboration-focused, Self-hosting requires technical effort, Mobile experience can require additional setup.

Best for: Developers, researchers, and power users managing large personal knowledge bases.

What Does Self-Hosting Actually Mean?

Self-hosting sounds intimidating until you break it down. Normally, when you use a cloud productivity service, the company's infrastructure stores and serves your data. With self-hosting, you run the service yourself.

That server might be a home server, a NAS, a VPS, a dedicated machine, a company server, or a private cloud environment. For example, AFFiNE officially supports deploying its knowledge base on your own infrastructure with Docker. AppFlowy also supports self-hosted deployment, including environments where organizations control infrastructure, storage, authentication, and backups.

Do You Actually Need Self-Hosting? Probably not. If you're a student who simply wants private offline notes, local-first software is usually enough. If you're a developer who wants complete infrastructure control, a company handling sensitive internal documentation, or someone running a homelab, self-hosting becomes much more attractive.

The important thing to remember is that self-hosting transfers responsibility to you. You become responsible for backups, updates, server security, authentication, storage, availability, and recovery when something breaks. That's the price of ownership.

Open Source vs. Privacy-Focused: Don't Confuse the Two

One detail gets overlooked constantly: open source does not automatically mean private. An application can publish its source code while still offering a cloud service that stores your information on external infrastructure. Likewise, a closed-source application can have strong privacy protections.

When evaluating a Notion alternative, look at the whole architecture:

  • Where is my data stored?
  • Can I use the application without an account?
  • Does it work offline?
  • Can I export my information?
  • Can I run the server myself?
  • Is synchronization encrypted?
  • Who controls the encryption keys?

My Practical 2026 Shortlist & Testing Guide

If I were moving away from Notion today, I would narrow the testing phase to four applications first:

  • Start with AppFlowy if... You want the closest overall transition from Notion with databases, projects, pages, and collaboration.
  • Start with AFFiNE if... You work visually and need a combination of documents, databases, and whiteboards.
  • Start with Anytype if... Your primary question is "Where does my data actually live?" and you want encrypted peer-to-peer architecture.
  • Start with Logseq if... You want daily notes, research, backlinks, ideas, and an outliner-first knowledge graph.

How I Would Test These Apps Before Switching

Don't migrate your entire Notion workspace on day one. That is how you end up spending an entire weekend importing thousands of pages only to discover that you hate the new editor. Instead, create the same small test workspace in each application.

  1. Test 1: Daily Notes — Create seven days of notes. Check writing speed, search, backlinks, templates, and offline access.
  2. Test 2: A Small Database — Create a simple project tracker with Project, Status, Deadline, Priority, and Notes to see whether the database workflow feels natural.
  3. Test 3: Offline Mode — Turn off Wi-Fi. Try to open existing notes, create a new page, edit an existing page, add an attachment, reconnect, and check synchronization.
  4. Test 4: Export — Before committing, export your test data. A productivity application should not become your digital prison.
  5. Test 5: Real-World Friction — Use the app for a week—not an hour. The first impression can be misleading.

Final Recommendation

There isn't one perfect replacement for Notion because Notion itself covers several different categories at once. If you want the most familiar transition, start with AppFlowy. If your workflow combines writing with visual planning, give AFFiNE a serious test. If privacy, encryption, offline-first operation, and local ownership are your priorities, Anytype deserves a place near the top of your shortlist.

If you're a researcher, developer, or knowledge worker who naturally thinks in interconnected bullets and references, Logseq may fit you better than a conventional Notion clone. For specialized use cases, SiYuan and Trilium Notes are both powerful choices—particularly when you care more about building a deep personal knowledge base than having a polished team workspace. And Focalboard remains interesting for its Kanban-focused approach, but its current maintenance status makes it a very different proposition.

The biggest lesson is simple: don't choose an alternative because it has the longest feature list. Choose it based on where you want your data to live and how you actually think and work. For a privacy-conscious Notion user in 2026, that's the comparison that matters most.
